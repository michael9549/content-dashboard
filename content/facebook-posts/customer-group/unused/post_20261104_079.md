A compass that is slightly wrong can still look useful at the beginning of a journey.

The error becomes obvious only after enough distance has passed.

Trading mistakes often work that way. A stop is moved once and the position recovers. An impulsive entry produces a profit. Risk is increased and the account gets away with it. The immediate outcome can hide a crooked process.

That is why learning from mistakes requires more than looking at profit and loss.

A winning trade can contain a serious mistake. A losing trade can reflect excellent execution. When outcome becomes the only teacher, luck gets mistaken for skill and good discipline gets abandoned because uncertainty produced a loss.

The better review focuses on alignment. Did the action match the written plan? Was the setup present before entry? Was risk acceptable from the start? Was the exit based on market information or emotional discomfort?

Mistakes also need categories. A knowledge mistake means the trader misunderstood the setup. An execution mistake means the setup was understood but not followed. An emotional mistake means a feeling overruled a known rule. Each category needs a different correction.

More education may solve a knowledge gap. A checklist may solve an execution gap. Smaller size, a pause, or a daily stop may solve an emotional gap. Treating every mistake with the same vague promise to do better leaves the compass unchanged.

The most valuable mistakes are converted into visible systems. If late entries keep recurring, define the maximum distance from the planned level. If revenge trades appear after losses, create a mandatory break. If oversized positions show up during confidence streaks, lock risk to a fixed percentage.

The goal is not to become a trader who never makes mistakes. That goal creates denial. The goal is to notice errors sooner, reduce their cost, and prevent repetition from turning them into identity.

A compass can be recalibrated, but only after someone admits the direction is off.
