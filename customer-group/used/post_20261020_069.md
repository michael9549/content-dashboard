A cracked compass is dangerous only when nobody checks where it points.

Trading mistakes become dangerous in the same way. A single error does not define a trader. An unexamined error can quietly guide dozens of future decisions in the wrong direction.

The natural response to a mistake is often shame or justification. Shame says the trader should have known better. Justification says the market made the mistake unavoidable. Neither response improves the process. Useful learning requires a more neutral question: What happened between the plan and the action?

Perhaps the entry was chased because a previous move was missed. Perhaps size increased because the last two trades won. Perhaps the stop moved because accepting the loss felt worse than increasing the risk. The chart shows where the trade occurred, but honest review reveals why.

A strong journal captures both. It records the setup, numbers, and market conditions, then adds the decision context. Over time, repeated patterns become visible. The trader may discover that most rule breaks happen late in the session, after social media use, or immediately following a loss. That observation turns a vague weakness into a solvable problem.

The next step should be specific. If late-session trades cause trouble, establish a cutoff time. If position size changes with emotion, calculate it before entry. If losing trades trigger revenge, require a ten-minute reset. A lesson is not complete until it changes a future behavior.

Review should also recognize correct decisions. If the process was followed and the trade lost, that is not a mistake to eliminate. It is evidence that uncertainty remains even when execution is sound.

A compass can be recalibrated, but only after the error is measured. Traders improve faster when mistakes become data instead of identity.
