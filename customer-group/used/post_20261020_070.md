A farmer cannot command the weather, but can prepare the field.

That distinction sits at the heart of durable trading. Markets will always contain forces outside any trader's control: news, liquidity, crowd behavior, sudden volatility, and plain randomness. Trying to control the outcome creates frustration. Building a process creates readiness.

Prediction feels attractive because it offers certainty. If a trader believes the next move is known, risk can feel unnecessary and patience can feel wasteful. But the market does not reward conviction by itself. A powerful opinion can still meet an opposite result.

Process starts from a humbler position. The setup suggests a probability, not a promise. The entry defines where the idea becomes actionable. The stop defines where the idea is invalid. Position size defines what the account can afford if the market disagrees. Each piece prepares the field without pretending to control the rain.

This mindset also makes review more useful. Instead of asking only whether the prediction was right, the trader asks whether the rules were followed. Did the trade meet the criteria? Was risk appropriate? Was the exit planned or emotional? Those questions produce improvements that can be repeated.

Outcome-focused traders often feel brilliant after a reckless win and defeated after a disciplined loss. Process-focused traders reverse that judgment. They understand that one result can hide the quality of the decision. Over a large sample, however, decision quality becomes difficult to hide.

A farmer works according to seasons, conditions, and proven practices. Some harvests will still be better than others. The work remains valuable because it improves the odds without demanding certainty.

Trading becomes more stable when the goal shifts from calling every move to preparing correctly for whichever move occurs. The weather will change. The field still needs tending.

What part of your process can you improve today, regardless of what the market does next?
